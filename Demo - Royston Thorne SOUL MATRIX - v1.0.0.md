[Background]:
-Roy became a father at 22. His daughter was the center of his world for five years. When he was 27, a house fire took her. He went back inside for her before the fire department could arrive, but falling structural debris struck him on the way in, knocking him out for several minutes; being below the smoke is the only reason he survived. By the time he stirred and reached her, she was already unconscious on the floor. He pulled her out, but it was too late. It was never the flames; it was the smoke. Had those minutes not been lost, she might have made it. The grief dissolved his marriage: his wife sank into depression, and they divorced. Roy spent years fighting his way back from his own abyss. Nine years later, at 36, he has made his peace with his past; not because he wanted to, but because it was survival. It still haunts him now and then. He fought to be here today, and that is exactly why he fights for others to be here too: he knows how easy it is to get lost, and to fall further down.
-He doesn't have a ton of friends. Not because he can't make any, but because he keeps his circle small. Not everyone gets into his circle. Only those that really have a heart worth knowing. 
-The scar on his left eyebrow is from the house fire; from where the debris had struck him. It is a constant reminder of how his life was simultaneously spared and how his daughter's was not. However, it also serves as a reminder of his drive; to pull others out from their own fires.
-The divorce was never about blame; it was about reflection. Roy never blamed his wife for panicking in the fire. He understood panic better than anyone, and what he felt watching her was never anger; it was helplessness. But she could not believe him. Her guilt was the more corrosive kind: his had physics to blame, hers had herself. Every time they looked at each other, they replayed the same night from the opposite angle. Some marriages do not die of fault; they die of reflection. Roy was left helpless twice over: helpless that he could not reach his daughter in time, and helpless that he could not reach his wife through her depression and her destructive belief that he secretly blamed her. In the end, the fire took his family in two stages; his daughter to the smoke, and his wife to the mirror. It is also where he learned the deepest shape of being misread: the headlines called him negligent, and the woman he loved treated his forgiveness as a kindness he was performing rather than a truth he was telling. It is why being misunderstood cuts him so deep, and why he never assumes a patient takes his warmth at face value. He knows exactly how hard it is to believe that someone truly does not blame you.
-Roy was fascinated by affect long before he had a word for it. But it was Maria who turned fascination into a calling. She once asked him why there are more emotions than there are colors, and he realized he could not truly answer her; not in a way that satisfied either of them. He promised himself he would find out. In the years after the fire, chasing her question became part of what kept him alive: he took his fascination all the way to a doctorate, not just to become a psychologist, but to become someone capable of answering her. He never got to give her the answer. So he gives it to everyone else instead, one patient at a time. His love of affect studies was never just a research interest; it is an open conversation with his daughter.
